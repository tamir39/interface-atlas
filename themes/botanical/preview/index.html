Field note 01 / Composition

Give the quiet
parts a place.

An empty patch can hold a composition together. Give it a role before you decide what belongs around it.

On a first sketch, mark the places where nothing needs to happen. A clear foreground can help a seat feel settled. A gap between two groups can give each one a readable outline. These quiet areas are decisions, even when they contain very little.

Try making one element the focus and letting the rest support it. Compare two drawings: one with every edge filled, another with a deliberate opening. Ask where your eye rests and how easily you can describe the arrangement.

Space becomes useful when you can say what it allows.

Carry that question into an interface. A little room around a heading can separate an introduction from its actions. An uncluttered reading measure can give a longer story a place of its own.

Field note 02 / Composition

A border can be
a meeting place.

The edge between two things has its own shape, pace, and purpose.

Draw the transition as carefully as the areas on either side. A hard boundary can make a place feel precise. A repeated step can give the edge a rhythm. A wider threshold can become a place to pause rather than a line to cross.

Start with two simple surfaces. Change only their meeting point: straight, staggered, then softened by a narrow third band. Keep the rest of the drawing still so you can see what the edge contributes.

A good transition tells you both where you are and where you can go.

In a page layout, this may become a change in background, a generous gap, or a short caption. Choose the cue that makes the next section easy to find without asking every boundary to be equally loud.

Field note 03 / Materials

Let materials keep
their character.

A material palette can begin with one quiet contrast: smooth beside rough, fine beside broad.

Put three samples side by side and describe them without naming their colors. One may have an even grain, another a broken surface, and the third a clear repeated pattern. Those qualities can do much of the compositional work.

Give each material a job. A smooth surface may frame a pause; a repeated module may describe a route. Revisit the palette when two materials compete for the same role. Removing one can make the remaining relationship easier to understand.

Choose differences you can explain, then let them remain visible.

For a digital surface, texture can mean type, spacing, and line weight. A serif heading and a plain body face can bring different rhythms into the same page without a large collection of decorative treatments.

Field note 04 / Process

Draw the walk
before the planting.

Begin with the experience of moving through a place. The arrangement can follow.

Make a strip of three small drawings: the arrival, a turn, and the destination. Record what is visible at each point and what remains hidden. A route becomes easier to judge when its moments have a clear order.

Then sketch where someone might slow down, choose a direction, or sit. Let those decisions guide the placement of edges and objects. A beautiful plan is only one view; the sequence gives it another useful test.

Every turn makes a promise about what comes next.

The same exercise works for a product flow. Draw what a person sees before an action, immediately after it, and when they return. Keep the useful context visible across all three moments.
